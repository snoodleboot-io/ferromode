Examples & Visualizations

Physics-based synthetic signals modelled on well-known reference datasets. Each sample is event-rich — designed to show what EMD actually does when something interesting happens. All datasets include inline references. Run python scripts/generate_sample_data.py to regenerate.

Example 1: ECG — Arrhythmia Detection

1000 samples · 250 Hz · 4 seconds. Five normal sinus beats followed by a premature ventricular contraction (PVC) — the classic arrhythmia target for EMD-based clinical detectors. Superimposed: 0.3 Hz respiratory baseline wander, 50 Hz power-line artifact, and EMG noise. EMD isolates each component without a pre-specified filter bank.

Reference: Signal morphology from the McSharry dynamical ECG model (McSharry et al., IEEE Trans Biomed Eng 50(3):289, 2003). Validated against MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database (Goldberger et al., Circulation 101(23):e215, 2000 · physionet.org/content/mitdb).

ECG Decomposition · 250 Hz · 1000 samples · 1 PVC event
Signal: 5 normal beats + 1 PVC (beat 4) + baseline wander + 50 Hz artifact · IMF 1: 50 Hz power-line + EMG noise · IMF 2: QRS complex and beat-to-beat variation · IMF 3: P- and T-wave envelope · Residual: respiratory baseline drift

Example 2: Seismic — P / S / Surface Wave Separation

2000 samples · 100 Hz · 20 seconds. A teleseismic waveform with three distinct arrival phases: P-wave onset at 5 s (9 Hz, sharp), S-wave coda at 8 s (3.5 Hz, larger amplitude), and a Rayleigh + Love surface wave train at 12 s (0.35–0.5 Hz, very large amplitude and slow decay). Standard filter-bank approaches require knowing the wave speeds in advance. EMD extracts each phase adaptively.

Reference: Envelope characteristics from Boore & Atkinson (Earthquake Spectra 24(1):99, 2008 · doi:10.1193/1.2830434). Modelled on 2011 Tōhoku Mw 9.1 teleseismic record (IRIS Earthquake Science Center / USGS).

Seismic Decomposition · 100 Hz · 2000 samples · P + S + surface waves
Signal: Pre-event noise | P-wave @5s | S-wave @8s | surface wave train @12s · IMF 1: P-wave high-frequency content (6–12 Hz) · IMF 2: S-wave coda (2–6 Hz) · IMF 3: Surface wave envelope (0.2–1 Hz) · Residual: long-period static displacement
PythonCopyimport numpy as np import ferromode_py as fm signal = np.loadtxt('data/seismic_sample.csv') # CEEMDAN gives cleaner phase separation on non-stationary arrivals emd_cfg = fm.EmdConfig(max_imfs=5, sd_threshold=0.2) ensemble_cfg = fm.EnsembleConfig(num_ensembles=100, noise_std=0.2, seed=42) result = fm.ceemdan(signal, ensemble_cfg, emd_cfg) imfs = result.imfs().imfs() residue = result.imfs().residue() print(f"Separated {result.imfs().n_imfs()} wave components") # Identify arrivals by dominant frequency fs = 100.0 for i, imf in enumerate(imfs): n = len(imf) freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, 1.0 / fs) dom_f = freqs[np.argmax(np.abs(np.fft.rfft(imf * np.hanning(n))))] energy = np.sum(imf ** 2) print(f" IMF {i+1}: dom freq = {dom_f:.2f} Hz energy = {energy:.4f}")

What to Look For

  • IMF 1: P-wave high-frequency burst at t=5s — sharp onset, fast decay
  • IMF 2: S-wave coda at t=8s — lower frequency, ~3× larger amplitude than P
  • IMF 3: Surface wave train at t=12s — very slow, dominant energy in the record
  • Residual: Static permanent displacement (coseismic offset)
  • Geophysical use: IMF arrival times give P-S delay → hypocenter distance without bandpass filters

Example 3: Speech — Vowel / Stop / Vowel Decomposition

2000 samples · 16 kHz · 125 ms. A VCV token /ɑ-t-ɑ/ (as in "ata") — voiced vowel, stop closure, burst, vowel release. The signal switches between quasi-periodic (voiced) and aperiodic (burst/noise) regimes mid-window: exactly the scenario where Fourier-based methods fail. Formant structure: F1 = 768 Hz, F2 = 1333 Hz, F3 = 2534 Hz (male /ɑ/), F0 = 127 Hz fundamental.

Reference: Formant statistics from Hillenbrand et al. (J Acoust Soc Am 97(5):3099, 1995). Phoneme boundaries modelled on TIMIT corpus (Garofolo et al., LDC 1993 · LDC93S1).

Speech Decomposition · 16 kHz · 2000 samples · /ɑ-t-ɑ/ VCV token
Signal: Voiced /ɑ/ (0–45 ms) | stop closure | burst @65 ms | vowel release (70–125 ms) · IMF 1: High-frequency formant content + burst transient · IMF 2: Mid-frequency formants (F2/F3 range) · IMF 3: F1 and glottal pulse envelope · Residual: fundamental (F0) contour

Example 4: S&P 500 Equity Decomposition

502 trading days · ~2 years. A GARCH(1,1) synthetic index modelled on S&P 500 behaviour during the 2020 COVID-19 market dislocation: 200-day bull market → rapid -34% crash over 22 sessions → volatile recovery → new-high regime. Four distinct volatility regimes, one catastrophic event. This is the signal type where Fourier decomposition is essentially useless (non-stationary, non-linear, fat tails) and EMD was literally designed for.

IMF 1: Daily microstructure noise and bid-ask bounce · IMF 2: Short-term momentum (days–weeks) · IMF 3: Intermediate cycle — the crash and recovery arc · Residual: Secular trend.

Reference: Nugent C (2018). S&P 500 stock data. Kaggle. kaggle.com/datasets/camnugent/sandp500. Crash morphology and GARCH parameters calibrated against S&P 500 index Feb–Apr 2020 (peak -33.9% drawdown over 23 sessions).

Financial Decomposition · S&P 500 · 502 daily samples · crash @day 200
Signal: Bull market (d0–200) | -34% crash (d200–222) | recovery (d222–350) | new highs (d350–502) · IMF 1: Daily noise and microvolatility · IMF 2: Short-term momentum and mean-reversion · IMF 3: The crash-and-recovery arc · Residual: Secular price trend · GARCH(1,1) · ref: Kaggle S&P 500 / Nugent 2018
PythonCopyimport numpy as np import pandas as pd import ferromode_py as fm # Load S&P 500 data (Kaggle: camnugent/sandp500) df = pd.read_csv( "data/sp500/individual_stocks_5yr/AAPL_data.csv", parse_dates=["date"], index_col="date", ) prices = df["close"].values log_returns = np.diff(np.log(prices)) # ICEEMDAN: best mode separation on non-stationary financial series emd_cfg = fm.EmdConfig(max_imfs=6, sd_threshold=0.005) ens_cfg = fm.EnsembleConfig(num_ensembles=100, noise_std=0.2, seed=0) result = fm.iceemdan(log_returns, ens_cfg, emd_cfg) imfs = result.imfs().imfs() residue = result.imfs().residue() print(f"Extracted {result.imfs().n_imfs()} IMFs") for i, imf in enumerate(imfs): var_pct = np.var(imf) / np.var(log_returns) * 100 print(f" IMF {i+1}: {var_pct:.1f}% of total variance") # Denoised trend: residual + two lowest-frequency IMFs denoised = residue + imfs[-1] + imfs[-2] corr = np.corrcoef(log_returns, denoised)[0, 1] print(f"Trend correlation with original: {corr:.4f}")

Boundary Methods Showcase

How a boundary condition treats the signal edges directly shapes every envelope — and therefore every IMF. Ferromode ships six production-ready strategies. The comparison below runs all four general-purpose methods on the same four-sinusoid synthetic (32 + 16 + 8 + 2 Hz @ 256 Hz) so the ground truth is unambiguous.

Boundary Condition Comparison · 32+16+8+2 Hz synthetic · Signal, IMF 1–3, Residual
Each column shows the same signal decomposed with a different boundary method. ExtremasMirror and Periodic produce the cleanest frequency separation on this synthetic. MirrorEven is nearly identical. WaveformMatching performs well when recurring motifs exist in the interior.

ExtremasMirror — default

Reflects the signal's own extrema outward past each edge: the two outermost maxima (and minima) are mirrored symmetrically about the endpoint. Because only the extrema used for spline fitting are extended — not the full signal — the envelope hugs the true amplitude right to the boundary with minimal ringing. This is the default for good reason: it performs well on most real-world signals with no configuration required.

MirrorEven

Appends a full time-reversed copy of the signal on each side: [ signal_reversed | signal | signal_reversed ]. The even (palindrome) extension guarantees the value is continuous at every seam, suppressing the artificial extrema that a hard-clip boundary would introduce. Slightly more conservative than ExtremasMirror — good choice when you want a clean, symmetric extension without any frequency assumptions.

Zhao-Huang Mirror+Circular — periodic signals

Implements the mirror-extending method of Zhao & Huang (2001). The signal is extended as [ signal | signal_reversed ] — a 2N palindrome — and the envelope spline is fitted with a circular (cyclic tridiagonal) end condition that enforces equal first and second derivatives at the seam. Evaluation wraps modulo the period so no extrapolation ever occurs outside the knot range.

Reference: Zhao J-P & Huang D-J (2001), "Mirror extending and circular spline function for empirical mode decomposition method", Journal of Zhejiang University-SCIENCE 2(3):247–252.

WaveformMatching

Searches the interior of the signal for waveform segments that best match the shape near each boundary, then splices those matched segments outward as the extension. Unlike mirror or AR methods, it makes no assumption about the functional form of the signal — it simply finds the most similar-looking passage in the data and uses it to continue the signal past the edge. Particularly effective when the signal has recurring motifs (e.g. heartbeats, seismic codas, periodic transients).

Example 5: Wolf Annual Sunspots — Schwabe Cycle Extraction

288 annual observations · 1700–1987. The Wolf sunspot number series is one of the longest continuous geophysical records ever compiled, and the canonical benchmark for adaptive time-frequency analysis. The dominant ~11-year Schwabe cycle sits on top of longer-period modulations (Gleissberg ~80-year, Suess ~200-year) and an irregular residual trend — exactly the multi-scale, non-stationary structure EMD was designed to separate.

The Zhao-Huang periodic boundary is the natural choice here: the record covers ~26 complete Schwabe cycles, so the palindrome extension correctly wraps the dominant periodicity at both edges without spurious end effects.

What to Look For

  • IMF 1: The ~11-year Schwabe cycle — the heartbeat of solar activity, cleanly isolated
  • IMF 2: ~22-year Hale cycle — the polarity reversal period, visible as amplitude modulation of IMF 1
  • IMF 3: ~80-year Gleissberg secular variation — grand maxima and minima (Maunder minimum @1650–1715 shows up in residual)
  • Residual: Multi-century background trend
  • Why periodic boundary: The palindrome extension wraps the 11-year dominant cycle correctly at both the 1700 and 1987 edges, preventing the ringing that mirror-only methods produce on long cyclic records
